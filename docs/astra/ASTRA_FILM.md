# ASTRA

## The Quiet Room

### A film in second person

*Craft framework: Serge Shima — [github.com/smixs/visual-skills](https://github.com/smixs/visual-skills) (CC BY 4.0). Dramaturgy, detail law, five anchors and shot discipline applied throughout.*

---

## 1. THE ROOM — 6:12 PM

You are Elias Vance, and you are forty-six years old, and you are standing in the doorway of the only room in the house that is truly yours.

The room is small enough that the door almost touches the desk when it opens. The walls are the colour of old paper. The floor is a rug worn pale in the middle, where your feet go. The window is a tall sash, paint flaking at the latch, and beyond it the city is turning on its lights one window at a time.

It is dusk. The sky is doing the thing it does in November — a low band of apricot under a lid of deep blue, the colour of a peach left too long on a windowsill. Rain has just stopped. The street below is black and wet and holds the streetlamps in long trembling pieces.

The room is full of small, specific things.

Bookshelves built from scaffold boards and brick. Paperbacks stacked two deep. A hardback lying open, face-down, spine bent — a crime you will forgive yourself for. A fern that has survived three flats and one heartbreak. Three jars on the sill with cuttings rooting in cloudy water. A candle that is mostly wax. A tin of foreign coins. A sea-glass marble. A grey plastic dinosaur, missing a horn.

Above the desk, a photograph in a wooden frame: you at nine, sitting at a beige computer, your mother beside you — round glasses, kind eyes, one hand on your shoulder. On the glass, in her handwriting:

*The web is a library, Eli. Don't let them turn it into a mall.*

The desk is an old kitchen table, scarred with pale rings where hot mugs have stood. On it: a chipped mug, a jam jar of pens, reading glasses with one arm mended in tape, a stack of letters you keep meaning to answer, and a small black notebook with TODAY written on the cover in silver marker.

You hang your coat on the back of the door. You kick off your shoes. You cross the rug in three steps.

In the laundry basket at the end of the desk, a grey cat named Miso is asleep with one paw over its face. Miso does not move. Miso has seen you come home before.

You put the kettle on the little hob in the corner. While it boils, you stand at the window and look at the sky. A single meteor falls through the last of the light — thin as a hair, gone before you can wish on it.

You make tea. You sit. The chair sighs.

You open the black notebook.

**TODAY**

1. Finish the Haddad translation. (Page 61 of 90.)
2. Call Nadia.
3. Water the fern.

You look at the list for a long moment. Then you close the notebook, put on your headphones, and lower the needle onto the record that is already waiting — Joni Mitchell, side B. The crackle fills the room like a fire being lit.

You press the power button on the tower under the desk.

## 2. THE OLD BROWSER — 6:19 PM

The fan wakes first. Then the monitor. The room splits into two kinds of light: the last amber from the window, the cold blue from the screen.

Your browser opens.

A bubble: **Restore pages? Chrome didn't shut down correctly.**

You click Restore. Forty-seven tabs pour in like a crowd through a narrow door. The fan climbs. The machine thinks about it.

You click the archive tab.

A cookie banner unfolds across the top of the page.

**We value your privacy. We and our 847 partners store and access information on your device…**

There is a big blue **Accept all**. There is a small grey **Manage preferences**. There is no **Reject all** anywhere you can see.

You click Manage preferences. Forty-three toggles, all on. A tab called **Legitimate interest** explains, in nine hundred words, why it is allowed to ignore you. At the bottom, in six-point grey: **Reject all.**

You click it. The banner folds away. The page reloads. The banner is back.

You click Reject all again. It accepts. A newsletter modal slides up: **Get 10% off your first order.** You close it. An autoplay video starts in the corner — a man shouting about a mattress, sound on. You mute it. You close it. It returns, smaller, in the corner.

You try to log in. Password field. You type from memory. Wrong. You try the saved one. A prompt: **For your security, confirm it's you. We've sent a code to your phone.**

Your phone is upstairs, charging, at four percent.

You go upstairs. You come back. You type the code. It expires while you type because you mistyped the first digit. You request another. A CAPTCHA: **Select all squares with traffic lights.** You select them. It asks again. You select the same squares, plus one half-square. It lets you through.

You get in.

Then the tab crashes.

**Aw, snap! Something went wrong while displaying this webpage.**

You stare at the screen. The record crackles. Miso shifts in the basket and settles.

You open a new tab. A bar at the top of the browser says:

**This extension may soon no longer be supported. Remove or replace it.**

It is your ad blocker.

You click Learn more. An article opens: *Chrome is ending support for Manifest V2 extensions.* You read three paragraphs. You understand maybe half. What you understand is enough: the thing that made the web quiet is being switched off.

You look at the clock. **6:38 PM.**

You have been home for twenty-six minutes. You have not translated a word.

## 3. THE LIST — 6:41 PM

You do the thing you always do when the machine wins: you ask the machine for help.

You type: **best browser 2026**.

The results bloom. The first link is a listicle, clean and confident, with a number in the title and a photograph of nine logos arranged like a family.

**The 9 Best Browsers of 2026, Tested by Humans.**

You click it.

You read standing up, one hand on the back of the chair.

1. Chrome
2. Edge
3. Firefox
4. Brave
5. Vivaldi
6. Opera
7. Arc
8. Zen
9. Safari

The article says each one is "the best" at something. Chrome is "the best for compatibility." Edge is "the best for productivity." Firefox is "the best for privacy." Brave is "the best for ad blocking." Vivaldi is "the best for power users." Opera is "the best for built-in tools." Arc is "the best for design." Zen is "the best for calm." Safari is "the best for Apple."

You look at the list for a long moment.

Then you do something you have not done since you were twenty-five and had a Saturday to lose: you decide to try them all. In order. One by one. Like a man trying on shoes.

You sit down. You open the notebook. Under **TODAY**, you write a fourth thing:

**4. Find a browser that gets out of the way.**

Then you close it and begin.

## 4. CHROME — 6:44 PM

You do not install Chrome. Chrome is already there, like a houseguest who never left.

You clear the deck. You uninstall the extensions you do not use. You turn off the prompts you can find. You open twelve tabs.

The fan climbs. A yellow bar appears: **This page is using significant memory.**

You open the settings. **Privacy and security. Ad privacy.** A panel explains that Chrome can show you ads based on topics it has learned about you. There is a switch. You turn it off. Another switch appears: **Site-suggested ads.** You turn that off too.

You go back to the archive. The page loads. The cookie banner is back. The autoplay video is back. The 847 partners are back.

You look at the address bar. It is a long string of numbers and question marks and `utm_source` and `gclid` and something called `msclkid`. The URL is twice as long as the page's name.

You close Chrome.

You open the software centre and click **Remove**. The icon disappears from the dock.

One down.

---

## 5. EDGE — 6:58 PM

You download the Edge package. The software centre asks for your password. You type it. A progress bar. **Install.**

Edge opens with a welcome screen the size of a small cinema.

**Welcome to Microsoft Edge.**

**Sign in to sync your favourites, passwords, and settings.**

**Import from Chrome.**

**Make Edge your default browser.**

**Personalise your news feed.**

You click **Not now** four times. You turn off the news feed. You turn off the shopping assistant. You turn off the coupon suggestions.

The browser settles. You open the archive.

The page is clean. The PDF opens in Edge's own viewer, smooth as glass. You click **Read aloud** and a calm voice begins to read the article to you. For one minute, you are happy. The voice is good. The PDF is good. You can annotate. You can search. You can sign.

Then a panel slides in from the right: **Compare prices for "Haddad translation"?**

You close it. Another: **Try the AI assistant.**

You close it. Another: **Make Edge your default browser?**

You look at the screen. The browser is not reading the page. It is reading you.

You close it. You open the software centre. **Remove.**

Two down.

---

## 6. FIREFOX — 7:16 PM

You install Firefox. The installer asks if you want to import from Chrome. You say yes. It asks if you want to make Firefox your default. You say not now. It asks if you want to sign in to sync. You say not now.

Firefox opens.

For a moment, you feel something like relief. The browser is not selling you a wallet. It is not asking you to sign in with a corporate account. It is a browser, made by people who seem to believe the web should belong to the people who use it.

You install uBlock Origin. The little shield turns red. The archive loads clean. The cookie banner is gone. The autoplay video is gone. The page is quiet.

You open a new tab.

The new tab is a grid of stories. A celebrity divorce. A listicle. A sponsored tile for a mattress. At the top, in small grey letters: **Recommended by Pocket.**

You stare at it.

The menus are the same grey boxes you remember from your mother's computer. The same words, the same order, the same little arrows. It is not bad. It is just old. It is the browser equivalent of a favourite coat that no longer fits across the shoulders.

You open the settings. You look for the thing that would let you keep work and home apart without opening a second browser. You find **Containers** — buried, technical, a feature for people who already know what it is.

You look for passkeys. Firefox can use the system authenticator, if the system has one. Your Linux desktop does not.

You look at the clock. **7:41 PM.**

You close Firefox. You do not uninstall it. You leave it in the dock, like a book you might come back to.

---

## 7. BRAVE — 7:44 PM

You install Brave. It asks if you want to import. You say yes. It asks if you want to make Brave your default. You say not now.

The browser opens. The shield in the address bar counts something. **12 trackers blocked.** You open the archive. The page is clean. The cookie banner is gone. The autoplay video is gone. The ad slots are empty rectangles that fold themselves away.

For a moment, you think: this is it. This is the one.

Then the toolbar shows a small orange triangle. **Brave Rewards.** A panel: **Earn tokens for viewing privacy-respecting ads.** Another: **Set up Brave Wallet.**

You close the panel. You open the settings. You turn off Rewards. You turn off the wallet. You turn off the crypto. You turn off the thing that asks you to turn things on.

The browser is quiet again. But there is still a wallet icon in the toolbar, small and patient, like a salesman waiting in the hall.

And underneath it all, the engine is Chromium. Google's engine. The same engine that is ending the ad blocker. The same engine that decides what the web is allowed to be.

You close Brave. You open the software centre. **Remove.**

Three down.

---

## 8. VIVALDI — 8:02 PM

You install Vivaldi. It opens with a tour.

**Tab stacks. Notes. Mail. Calendar. Panels. Quick commands. Mouse gestures.**

You like the tab stacks. You like the idea of a browser that respects the fact that you have forty-seven tabs and a life. You stack the archive tabs. You stack the news tabs. You stack the ones you are afraid to close.

Then you open the settings.

There are three hundred and twelve options. You count. You actually count. There are settings for the settings. There is a setting called **"Use Native Window"** and another called **"Use Native Window (Alt)"**. You do not know what either of them does. You are afraid to find out.

You find the tab stack. You find the notes. You find the mail. You find the calendar. You find a setting that changes the colour of the tab stack. You change it. You change it back.

The browser is a cockpit. You wanted a chair.

You close it. You open the software centre. **Remove.**

Four down.

---

## 9. OPERA — 8:19 PM

You install Opera. It opens with a start page full of promoted bookmarks. A shopping deal. A news story. A casino. You close them one by one. They come back.

There is a wallet. There is a VPN. The VPN is a proxy, and the proxy is free, and the free proxy is the product.

You open the settings. You find **Opera Ads**. You turn it off. You find **Promoted bookmarks**. You turn it off. You find the wallet. You turn it off.

You open the archive. The page loads. It is fine. It is a browser. It works.

But you cannot shake the feeling that you are not the customer. You are the shelf space.

You close it. You open the software centre. **Remove.**

Five down.

---

## 10. ARC — 8:36 PM

You click the Arc link.

The page is beautiful. Soft corners, a sidebar like a well-kept desk, a browser that looks like it was designed by someone who has been to a museum.

Then the download button:

**Arc for macOS and Windows.**

You are on Linux.

You scroll. You look for a Linux download. There isn't one. You look for a "notify me" button. There is. You do not click it.

You sit back. The record has ended. The room is quiet except for the rain starting again, soft against the glass.

Six down. Or five and a wall.

---

## 11. ZEN — 8:41 PM

You install Zen. It is a Flatpak, and it takes a while. The software centre asks for your password. You type it. The progress bar crawls.

Zen opens.

It is beautiful. Really beautiful. Vertical tabs. A calm, narrow sidebar. Split view. A workspace switcher. The new tab is a quiet gradient with a single field, no feed, no sponsored tiles.

You open the archive. The page loads. You split the view and put your manuscript beside it. It works.

For a moment, you are almost home.

Then you right-click a link. A Firefox menu appears — grey, ancient, a ghost in a new house.

You open the settings. **Firefox Sync.** **Sign in to sync your tabs, bookmarks, and passwords.** You close it.

You look for a built-in ad blocker. There isn't one. You install uBlock Origin. It works. You look for passkeys. Firefox's system authenticator. Your Linux desktop still does not have one.

You look at the browser. It is a beautiful coat. But when you put your hand in the pocket, you find someone else's keys.

You close it. You do not uninstall it. You leave it in the dock, next to Firefox, like two versions of the same almost.

Seven down. One wall. One almost.

---

## 12. THE LOW POINT — 9:04 PM

The room is dark now except for the monitor and the little lamp. The rain has settled into a steady hush. Miso has left the basket and is sitting on the windowsill, watching the street.

You look at the clock. **9:04 PM.**

You have been home for almost three hours. The translation is still on page sixty-one. Your tea is cold. Your back aches. Your eyes are dry.

You look at the notebook.

**TODAY**

1. Finish the Haddad translation. (Page 61 of 90.)
2. Call Nadia.
3. Water the fern.
4. Find a browser that gets out of the way.

You have done none of them. You have installed seven browsers. You have removed five. You have two almosts in the dock and a wall where Arc should be.

You look at the photograph of your mother.

*The web is a library, Eli. Don't let them turn it into a mall.*

You put your head in your hands.

**YOU**
*(barely audible)*
I know, Mum. I'm trying.

You are about to close the whole thing — the browser, the evening, the day — when your eye catches something at the bottom of the listicle. A comment, grey and small, buried under eleven replies.

**COMMENT**
*I've been using Astra for six months. It's a beta. Linux only. No sync. But it's the first browser in years that just… gets out of the way.*

You read it twice.

**YOU**
Astra.

You hover the link. The cursor hesitates.

Then you click.

## 13. ASTRA — 9:09 PM

The website is quiet.

No carousel. No cookie banner. No chat bubble. Just a sail mark on a pale ground, a sentence, and a download button.

**A calmer way to cross the web.**

You scroll. There is a table comparing browsers. Honest, almost blunt: **Beta. Linux x86_64. No sync yet. Accounts and AI surfaces out of scope.**

You appreciate that. You are tired of being sold to.

You click **Download**. A small progress bar. It does not ask for an email. It does not ask how you heard about them. It does not ask you to create an account.

It finishes.

**YOU**
*(surprised)*
That's it?

You extract the archive. A folder. An install script. You run it. It asks for nothing. A small sail icon appears in your applications.

You click it.

---

## 14. THE FIRST FRAME — 9:12 PM

The screen goes dark.

For a moment, nothing. Then a single sail, drawn in one line, in the centre of the black.

No spinner. No "loading your experience." No skeleton screens assembling themselves like scaffolding.

The sail fades.

And the browser is simply *there* — whole. The window frame, the sidebar, the address field, the clock in the corner. Finished, on the first frame, as if it had been waiting politely for you to look.

You lean in.

**YOU**
*(quiet)*
Oh.

You move the mouse. The interface responds like it heard you.

You right-click the empty page. A menu opens — Astra's own. Soft corners, your own words, your own order. No grey ghost.

You click **Settings**. It opens *inside a tab*, like a page, with the same type and the same air. Not a separate window from 2008. Not a control panel. A room in the same house.

You scroll slowly.

No **Sign in**. No **Sync**. No **Personalisation**. No **Telemetry**.

**YOU**
*(reading)*
There's no telemetry switch.

You keep scrolling. There is no telemetry at all. It is not off. It was never built.

Something in your chest loosens, just slightly.

There is a small **Beta** tag in the corner. You almost laugh.

**YOU**
At least you're honest.

## 15. THE SKY — 9:16 PM

You open a new tab.

And stop.

The sky fills the page — the actual sky, or something that remembers it. Deep blue at the top, a seam of amber at the bottom. The same colours as the window beside you. Frosted glass where the light falls.

In the middle, in a quiet serif:

**Good evening, Elias.**

Below it, the time. The date. And three small things, arranged like objects on a desk:

**Focus — 0 min of a 120 min goal**
**Today — 1 task**
**Trackers blocked — 0**

You look from the screen to the window. The sky on the screen and the sky outside are the same sky.

**YOU**
*(barely a whisper)*
You know it's evening.

You sit back. For the first time all day, you don't click anything. You just look.

Then you notice the small line under the greeting:

**Today — 1 task.** You click it.

A small panel opens. **Finish the Haddad translation.** Tomorrow's list is already there, waiting, because you wrote it in the notebook and typed it in without thinking.

You look at the notebook on the desk. Then at the screen. Then back.

**YOU**
*(soft)*
You're holding the day.

## 16. THE IMPORT — 9:21 PM

You click **Import**.

A simple question: **Where are your bookmarks?**

Three names, offered like old friends: **Chrome. Firefox. Zen.**

You select all three. A progress line. Then your bookmarks appear — years of them, folders and half-folders and things you saved at 2 a.m. and forgot. Nothing lost. Nothing uploaded. They just… arrive.

You scroll the list. Your eyes stop on a folder called **Mum**.

You don't open it. Not yet.

## 17. THE ARCHIVE — 9:27 PM

You go back to the research archive. You paste the address. You press Enter.

The page loads before you can blink. No stutter. No fan roar. The address bar has quietly cleaned itself — the long string of tracking parameters gone, the URL shorter and truer than the one you pasted.

In the corner of the address field, a small shield appears.

**23 trackers blocked.**

The cookie banner is gone. The autoplay video is gone. The mattress ad is a polite empty rectangle, and then the rectangle folds away entirely, like someone taking down a sign.

You scroll. The article is just… words. Black on white. A photograph. A caption.

**YOU**
*(soft)*
Hello.

You click **Sign in**. The archive asks for your passkey.

A small sheet slides up from the bottom of the window. Astra's own — not a grey dialog, not a browser prompt with a padlock icon from 2011. A simple field.

**Enter your PIN.**

You type six digits.

The sheet closes. You're in.

No phone. No code. No CAPTCHA. No coat at the office. Just you, your PIN, and a key that lives encrypted on your own machine, behind a lock only you can open.

You stare at the logged-in page.

**YOU**
*(to yourself)*
You kept it here.

## 18. THE WORK — 9:34 PM

You work.

And we watch you work, which is its own kind of quiet cinema.

You open the archive on the left. You drag your manuscript to the right. The window splits cleanly down the middle. You drag the divider. It moves. You drag it back. It stays where you put it.

You open a PDF from the archive. It doesn't launch another application. It opens in Astra, in a tab, with a page count and a search field. You search a name. It jumps to page fourteen. You close it and reopen it and it is still on page fourteen.

You hover a link in a footnote — and a small card floats up, showing you the page beneath: the title, the first lines, a glimpse. You read enough to know you don't need it. You move on. The tab was never opened.

You type into the task field: **Finish the Haddad translation by Thursday.**

The field reads it back to you as you type — *by Thursday* underlined, softly, like a teacher's pencil. The task appears with a due date. You didn't open a calendar. You didn't pick a date from a grid.

**YOU**
*(small smile)*
You understood me.

You start a focus session. A small ring fills in the corner. The time tracker begins, quietly, counting the minutes you actually spend in the archive — and nothing else.

## 19. THE EXTENSION — 9:49 PM

You need a citation tool. It only exists in the Chrome Web Store.

You almost give up. Then you remember the download page — *Firefox add-ons and Chrome packages, side by side.*

You search. You find it. You click **Add to Astra.**

A sheet appears: permissions, in plain language, with a signature check and a note that the package was verified. You read it. You click **Install**.

It works.

Then, because you can, you install a reading add-on from Firefox's store too. It works as well. Two stores. One browser. No second runtime, no compatibility mode, no "this extension may not work here."

You laugh — a real one, surprised out of you.

**YOU**
Both. You let me have both.

## 20. THE SPACES — 10:01 PM

You create a space.

**Work — The Translation.**

Then another: **Home.**

You drag a personal tab — a recipe, a gift for your sister — toward Home. It lifts out of Work and lands in Home and vanishes from the work window entirely. Not hidden. Not grouped. *Gone* from that life.

You open Home. Different bookmarks. Different history. Different cookies. The same browser, the same window, the same man — but a different room inside it.

You switch back to Work. Your forty-seven tabs are still there, exactly as you left them.

**YOU**
*(quietly amazed)*
You keep them apart.

## 21. THE SLEEPING — 10:16 PM

You notice the sleeping.

The tabs you haven't touched in a while have gone still. Not gone — *resting.* A small moon icon beside each one. You click one and it wakes instantly, scrolled to the exact paragraph you left, the exact place in the video, the exact half-written comment.

You open ten more tabs out of spite. The fan doesn't change. The machine doesn't care.

You open the settings and find the policy: **Sleep after 15 minutes. Never sleep these sites.** You add the archive to the never-sleep list.

**YOU**
*(to yourself)*
You let me choose.

## 22. THE PRIVATE WINDOW — 10:31 PM

You open a private window.

It is the same room, but the light is different — cooler, quieter. No sky. No greeting. No history.

You search for a gift for your sister. A necklace. Something small and silver. You browse for a while. You find it. You don't buy it yet.

You close the private window. You open your history. The gift is not there. The necklace does not exist. Nothing was written. Nothing was kept.

You sit back.

**YOU**
*(soft)*
You didn't remember.

It is the nicest thing a browser has ever done for you.

## 23. THE SHIELD — 10:44 PM

You open the shield menu on a news site that has been bothering you for years. A small panel: **Blocked on this site. Fingerprinting protection: Standard. Pause for this site.**

You pause it. The site relaxes — a video loads, an image appears. You watch for ten seconds, remember why you blocked it, and turn the shield back on. The page goes quiet again.

You open the fingerprinting settings. Three levels. Two independent spoofs — language, screen. A note: **Your user agent and timezone stay real, so sites keep working.**

You read it twice. It is the first privacy setting you've ever seen that admits what it doesn't do.

**YOU**
*(to the screen)*
You're not lying to me.

## 24. THE SCORE — 10:51 PM

You open the ad blocker test page. You don't know why. Curiosity, maybe. A score appears.

**443 / 443. Score: 100.**

You blink. You refresh. The same.

You laugh — louder this time, a little disbelieving.

**YOU**
A hundred.

You look at the window. The first star is still there, low and bright. You hadn't noticed it stay.

## 25. MUM — 11:02 PM

You go back to the bookmarks. The folder called **Mum**.

You hover over it. Your hand hesitates on the mouse.

Then you click.

Inside: a single bookmark. **Iris's Kitchen — Recipes & Notes.** A blog she kept for eleven years. You haven't opened it since she died.

You click it.

The page loads. It is old. It is slow in the way old things are slow, but Astra waits for it, patient. A header with a wooden spoon. A photograph of your mother, younger than you are now, holding a lemon.

And then the page settles.

No ads. No pop-ups. No **We value your privacy.** The blocker has stripped it all away — the sidebars, the trackers, the ghost of an ad network that bought the site years after she stopped writing. What is left is her.

Her words. Her handwriting in the recipe cards. A post from 2009: **Elias is home this weekend. Making the orange cake.**

You read it.

Your eyes go glassy.

**YOU**
*(whisper)*
Hi, Mum.

The cursor blinks beside her words. The room is very quiet. Outside, the rain has stopped for good. Miso jumps down from the windowsill and settles in your lap.

You sit there for a long time.

## 27. THE LAST PAGE — 11:41 PM

You write.

The manuscript is open on the right. The archive on the left. Your focus ring is nearly full. The task list has one thing crossed out: **Finish the Haddad translation.**
